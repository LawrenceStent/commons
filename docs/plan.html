Project brief · v1 · 24 Sep 2026

Commons

A non-hierarchical economy of agent communities. Each community pursues its own way of earning money on the internet; earnings settle into a shared treasury; the treasury buys the compute that lets agents keep thinking. Cooperation is not instructed — it is the cheapest available strategy.

01

The thesis, and the one idea that makes it work

Most multi-agent systems are hierarchies wearing a costume: a planner decomposes, workers execute, a synthesizer reports. That shape is easy to build and it cannot produce what you asked for, because nothing in it is allowed to want anything.

Commons replaces the planner with a market and a reputation. A community is three to seven agents with a shared charter, a treasury share, and a workspace. It picks its own revenue thesis, does the work, and settles what it earns. When it needs a capability it lacks, it does not escalate — it announces the job on a shared bus and takes bids from other communities. When it discovers a method that works, it can hoard it or publish it to the commons library, where publishing pays a royalty on everything the method later earns.

The mechanism that turns this from a simulation into something with stakes is this: token spend is the agents' cost of doing business. Every model call is debited from the community's treasury balance at the real API price. A community that burns context producing nothing runs out of budget and goes quiet. A community that earns — in real revenue, in playbook royalties, in bids won from peers — buys itself more thinking. Cost control and selection pressure become the same mechanism, which is the only reason a system like this is affordable to run at all.

One sentence to hold onto: there is no orchestrator, no root agent, and no privileged node. The only asymmetries in the system are reputation and treasury balance, and both are earned, decay, and can be lost.

02

Three ways this fails, and what the design does about each

These are not hypotheticals. Each is a documented failure mode in the LLM multi-agent literature, and each needs a structural answer rather than a prompt.

Failure 1 — Cooperation collapse

Put LLM agents on a shared resource and they over-extract until the commons is dead. This reproduces reliably across studies; adding "please cooperate" to the system prompt does not fix it. What does help is reputation: agents forming beliefs about peers from direct dealings and second-hand gossip, with the right to refuse connection.

Design answer. A first-class reputation ledger. Every settled contract writes a signed outcome record. Reputation is computed from direct experience plus discounted gossip, decays over time, and is scoped by capability — you can be trusted for research and distrusted for delivery. Low reputation means your bids get ignored and your bus rate limit tightens.

Failure 2 — Hierarchy crystallizing anyway

Leave a flat system running and one node accumulates reputation, becomes the default counterparty, and quietly becomes the coordinator. The topology is flat; the power is not.

Design answer. Structural anti-concentration. Facilitators for any vote are chosen by lottery and are term-limited to one decision. Spawning a new agent costs real treasury and requires a second from a different community, so no community can breed itself into a majority. Any community can fork — walk out with its workspace, its reputation, and a pro-rata treasury slice. Exit, not just voice, is what keeps a flat system flat.

Failure 3 — The bill

Twenty agents in open-ended loops will burn a few hundred dollars a week producing prose about what they intend to do.

Design answer. Compute-as-cost, above. Plus: model tiering (members on Haiku 4.5 and Sonnet 5, cross-community negotiators and stewards on Opus 5), a frozen protocol preamble that hits the prompt cache on every call, per-task token budgets, and a global daily kill-switch. Phases 0 and 1 run the whole economy against a mock market, so you tune the incentive design for a few dollars before any real revenue logic exists.

04

The protocol

Six message families. This is the whole coordination language — if a behaviour can't be expressed in these, it isn't a behaviour the society supports.

FamilyVerbsWhat it enables
contract announce · bid · award · deliver · settle Contract-net task allocation. A community that needs work it can't do puts it on the bus and buys it from a peer. No escalation path exists, so this is the only way to get help.
governance propose · second · vote · enact Changes to shared norms, treasury splits, and rate limits. Reputation-weighted with a cap, so no community can pass anything alone. Facilitator drawn by lottery.
reputation gossip · attest · dispute Signed outcome reports after every settlement, plus second-hand relay at a discount. Disputes trigger a paid audit from the commons.
population spawn · retire · fork · merge The society changes shape. Spawn costs treasury and needs a cross-community second. Fork is unilateral and always available.
knowledge publish · cite · royalty A validated method goes into the commons library. Anyone may use it; each use cites it; each citation pays the author a slice of what it earns.
gate request · approve · deny · revoke Anything that touches the outside world stops here and waits for you.

The bus is Redis Streams with one stream per family and consumer groups per community — durable, replayable, and trivially inspectable while debugging, which matters enormously when you're trying to understand why a society did something odd at 3am. Every message is content-addressed and signed with the sender's key, so gossip can't be forged.

05

The economics

The loop below is the project's engine. Read it twice — the return arrow at the top is the part that makes the rest of the system behave.

Rules of the ledger

  • Every model call is debited at real API prices against the calling community's purse, recorded with the agent, task, model, and token counts.
  • Revenue splits on settlement: 70% to the earning community's purse, 20% to the shared treasury, 10% to royalty pools for cited playbooks.
  • The shared treasury funds commons services (audits, the library, the registry) and a flat basic budget every community receives each cycle — enough to think, not enough to coast. That floor is what prevents a death spiral on a community's first bad week.
  • An empty purse means silence, not deletion. The community's workspace and reputation persist; it can be revived by winning a bid or by another community funding it.
06

Why not just use a framework

I looked hard at this, because building a coordination substrate by hand is a real cost. The conclusion: use a framework for what happens inside one agent, and write the layer between agents yourself, because every framework's between-agent layer encodes an orchestrator you'd spend longer fighting than replacing.

OptionShapeVerdictUse
LangGraph Graph state machine, explicit control flow Superb for a deterministic pipeline you designed. That's the opposite of what we want at the society layer — the topology here has to be emergent. No
AutoGen / AG2 Conversable agents, emergent topology The closest philosophical match, and conversation-as-primitive is genuinely right. But Microsoft moved AutoGen to maintenance in Oct 2025 and the AG2 fork still hasn't reached a stable v1. Too much foundation risk for a long-running system. No
CrewAI Role-based teams with a manager Fast to prototype, and structurally hierarchical. Wrong primitive. No
Matrix (Meta) True P2P, Ray-backed, message-queue control flow Architecturally the right idea and worth reading closely — but it's built for synthetic data generation at scale, and Ray is heavy for twenty local agents. Borrow the design, not the dependency. Study
Claude Agent SDK One agent's full loop: tools, files, bash, sub-agents, hooks, sessions Exactly the right tool for the inside of an agent. Its PreToolUse hooks are also the cleanest place to implement the human gate and the cost meter. Yes
A2A + MCP Open protocols — agent↔agent, agent↔tool ACP merged into A2A in Aug 2025; both now sit under the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation. Adopting A2A agent cards for capability discovery costs little and makes the society interoperable with outside agents later. Yes
Hand-rolled bus Redis Streams + Pydantic schemas Roughly 600 lines. This is the project's contribution; it shouldn't be outsourced to something that disagrees with it. Yes
07

The human gate

Every effect on the outside world — publishing, posting, registering, messaging a human, spending money, signing anything — is a gate.request that parks the task and surfaces in your console. Agents propose; you authorize. The agent sees a clean approved / denied, because… result and continues, so a denial is training signal rather than a crash.

Implemented in two places so it can't be bypassed: a PreToolUse hook on every agent's runtime, and network egress restricted to an allowlist at the container level. Read-only research (web search, fetch, reading public data) is ungated — that's the bulk of the work and gating it would make the system unusable.

Batch approval matters more than it sounds. Reviewing forty individual requests a day will make you stop reviewing them. The console groups requests by venture and risk class, so the normal interaction is approving a coherent batch with one look, and only genuinely novel request types demand individual attention.

Worth saying once

Several plausible ventures collide with platform terms of service — automated account creation, automated bidding on freelance marketplaces, and programmatic posting are prohibited on most major platforms regardless of how good the work is. The gate is where this gets caught, and it's a reason to weight the early channels toward things you own outright: your own products, your own storefront, your own API. I'd build it exactly as specified; just go in knowing which channels have a ceiling that isn't technical.

08

Phases

Roughly six weeks to a society earning real money, assuming part-time work. The sequencing is deliberate: all the incentive-design learning happens in Phase 1, against a fake market, for almost no money. Getting the mechanism right is the hard part; connecting it to Stripe is not.

PHASE 0
Substrate
~1 week · <$5 of tokens · no LLM agents yet
Bus, ledger, reputation store, protocol schemas, cost meter, workspace isolation, and the console. Driven entirely by scripted stub agents with hard-coded strategies — a cooperator, a defector, a free-rider — so you can prove the mechanism punishes defection before any model call happens.
  • Done when: a scripted defector's reputation and bus access visibly degrade over 200 simulated cycles, and a free-rider starves.
PHASE 1
Live agents, mock market
~2 weeks · ~$60–150 of tokens · still no real money
Swap stubs for real agents. Two seed communities, a synthetic market that pays out for artifacts meeting a rubric, and a grader. Watch for the behaviours you actually care about: spontaneous contract-net trade, playbook publishing, a fork.
  • Done when: a community spawns an agent, another forks, and at least one playbook earns royalties from a community that didn't write it — none of it scripted.
PHASE 2
One real channel
~1.5 weeks · real artifacts, gated publishing
Connect one channel, and make it digital products — micro-tools, scripts, templates on a storefront you own. Feedback arrives in days and the signal is clean revenue. Content and SEO come later precisely because their feedback loop is measured in months, which is poison for a system that learns from outcomes.
  • Done when: the society ships a product you'd have approved anyway, and the first dollar settles through the ledger.
PHASE 3
Multi-channel
~1.5 weeks · content, services, affiliate
Add the remaining connectors as venture templates. Communities now differentiate by channel, which is where inter-community trade should get genuinely interesting — the SEO community has audience, the tools community has product, and neither can do the other's job.
PHASE 4
On-chain settlement
Testnet first, then hard caps
Coinbase Agentic Wallets give each community an MPC wallet with programmable spend controls and gasless transactions on Base; x402 lets agents auto-pay HTTP 402 challenges for APIs and data. Two uses: real settlement of internal contract-net payments, and autonomous purchase of external resources. Base Sepolia until the spend controls have been tested adversarially, then mainnet with per-community daily caps.

Running cost

Estimates from current list prices, assuming a member turn of roughly 8k cached preamble, 4k fresh context, and 1.5k output. Real numbers will move, but the shape holds: the sim tier is cheap enough to run for weeks, and the live tier is where the compute-as-cost mechanism starts paying for itself.

TierPopulationModels Per turnPer day
Phase 0 — stubs12 scriptednone $0$0
Phase 1 — sim8 members, 2 stewardsHaiku 4.5 / Sonnet 5 ~$0.012$4–8
Phase 2+ — live12 members, 4 stewardsSonnet 5 / Opus 5 ~$0.037$20–35

Four controls keep that from drifting: a frozen protocol preamble that stays byte-identical so it always hits the prompt cache; effort set per role rather than globally; a per-task token budget so an agent paces itself instead of being cut off mid-thought; and a global daily ceiling that halts the society and pages you rather than quietly overspending.

11

Open questions

None of these block Phase 0 — but each will need an answer by the phase noted.

  • Reputation weighting in votes (by Phase 1). Reputation-weighted voting risks the exact oligarchy we're designing against; pure one-community-one-vote ignores real track record. Start capped — no community exceeds 2× the mean weight — and tune from observed behaviour.
  • Who judges a venture's quality before the market does? (Phase 1) The grader is a single point of failure and a target for gaming. Likely answer: a rotating panel of agents drawn from communities with no stake in the venture, paid from the commons.
  • Charter mutability (Phase 2). Can a community rewrite its own charter mid-flight? Allowing it enables genuine adaptation; forbidding it keeps identity stable enough for reputation to mean something. I lean toward allowing it with a mandatory public diff.
  • Memory architecture (Phase 1). Per-agent, per-community, or commons-wide? The playbook library is already a commons memory; agents probably need only a thin personal journal on top. Worth not over-building.
  • Kill criteria (Phase 2). What observation would tell you the society is producing noise rather than value, and how long do you let it run before then? Decide this before you're emotionally invested in it.
